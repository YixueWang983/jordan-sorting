\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion-summary}

This thesis reconstructs Simplified Jordan Sorting as one executable core with
ordinary-list and heterogeneous finger-tree backends. Both recover the sorted
output from their maintained state. The implementation connects the paper's
operations to a source-level cost argument and a reproducible runtime comparison.

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{sec:conclusion-questions}
\label{sec:conclusion-rq1}

\textbf{RQ1: reconstruction and correctness.}
The shared Step~1/2/3 loop maintains geometric ownership, exact sibling groups
and sorted prefixes. The argument in \cref{app:backend-argument} explains the
two odd-stage $z_1$ corrections, temporary transfer, reversed ribs and recursive
repair transients. Stable pair identities and child groups survive splitting.
All \UnifiedN{} inputs passed the experiment's equivalence and qualification
checks, and every saved output in the \UnifiedBatches{} formal sample batches
of the complete attempt agrees with its expected sorted order. These checks
support the reconstruction on the tested inputs without replacing its argument.

\label{sec:conclusion-rq2}
\textbf{RQ2: observed runtime.}
In the main experiment, the uninstrumented tree is faster on \UnifiedWins{} of \UnifiedN{} inputs;
the ordinary list is faster on the remainder. The input-level tree/list ratio
has median \UnifiedMedian{} and range \UnifiedMinimum{}--\UnifiedMaximum{}.
The pooled median decreases across the measured size bands, and the common
source's more heavily nested categories have lower ratios.
In the supplementary session, Python merge sort and quicksort both beat
the two Jordan backends on every input. For the tree, the median paired
time ratios were \BaselineTreeMerge{} against merge sort and
\BaselineTreeQuick{} against quicksort (\cref{tab:baseline-ratios}).

\label{sec:conclusion-rq3}
\textbf{RQ3: structure and cost.}
One potential pays propagation across mixed updates, giving amortized constant
endpoint insertion and smaller-side logarithmic split. A list-size potential
then pays the split terms over the full run, including bounded ownership and
shared-core work. The linear total uses unit-cost comparisons and machine-word
operations, expected dictionary lookup and amortized growth. Ordinary-list
insertion copying alone has a quadratic lower bound.

The targeted, structural, geometric and seeded sources include two-sided
splits. The largest tree advantages occur on heavily nested common inputs
with at most one split and no two-sided split. Within the shared
targeted/balanced/skew construction, ratios vary little with the selected
smaller-side parameter at a fixed length. Complete-sort measurements do not
identify which operations account for the tree's advantage.
The ordinary backend's parent-chain check, whose cost grows with nesting
depth, is one candidate (\cref{sec:limitations-timing-scopes}). How the proved
operation costs relate to measured runtime remains partly open over this
range (\cref{sec:limitations-timing-scopes}).

\section{Contributions and Further Work}
\label{sec:conclusion-contributions}
\label{sec:conclusion-future-work}
\label{sec:conclusion-closing}

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=.4\baselineskip]
  \item \textbf{Executable reconstruction.} The shared core implements the
  1990 algorithm with ordinary-list and finger-tree backends. It specifies
  the odd-stage $z_1$ handling, geometric endpoint rules and exact sibling-group
  invariant $G$ (\cref{chap:algorithm}).

  \item \textbf{Finite-parent locality.} We extend the published latest-child
  argument to the case where the latest member of an unchanged region is its
  finite parent. The first re-entry then uses the gap immediately inward from
  the parent's latest endpoint (\cref{lem:proof-reentry}).

  \item \textbf{Linear work bound.} The source-level argument establishes
  $O(n)$ expected/amortized work for the minimal core with the finger-tree
  backend on valid inputs under the stated computation model. A potential on
  tree nodes pays for repair propagation, and a potential on sibling-list
  sizes pays the aggregate split costs (\cref{thm:proof-total}).

  \item \textbf{Reproducible measurement.} The paired protocol qualifies
  inputs before timing and archives the batch records. Independent validation
  checks the saved outputs and recomputes the reported timing summaries
  (\cref{sec:method-qualification,sec:method-evidence}).

  \item \textbf{Observed runtime.} At every measured common-source length up
  to $n=\UnifiedMaxN{}$, the median tree/list ratio is lower in the more deeply
  nested categories. The tree's largest advantages occur in the heavy category,
  where no input performs a two-sided split
  (\cref{sec:results-structure,sec:results-nesting-by-length}).
\end{itemize}

Several extensions would test how far the observed trends carry. Generators
that combine depth with long sibling lists, and application-derived inputs
with large interior splits, would address structural gaps in the current
families. Open polygonal chains would add odd lengths, while multiple starts
and directions per polygon would test dependence on traversal choice.

Larger inputs, including the excluded length-1500 cases, would test whether
the narrowing of the tree/list ratio continues beyond $n=\UnifiedMaxN{}$.
Intersection sequences from real polygon data would connect the measurements
to the clipping application of Fung et al.\ Operation counts from the
instrumented tree could describe differences in work mix and guide targeted
measurements of candidate explanations. An ordinary-list variant with an
owner-only liveness check would separate representation-specific safety checks
from list-operation costs.

The two Jordan backends were timed on CPython.
Repeating the comparison on PyPy would test how just-in-time compilation
affects their relative speed and their performance against the Python
baselines. Two further experiments could measure how these comparisons change when the core tree operations are
implemented in C or Cython, or when node fields are stored in arrays to
reduce the number of Python objects allocated.

Independent review of the finite-parent locality extension and
the mixed-operation potential argument would strengthen confidence in the
cost analysis. Recognition, invalid-input behavior and error correction each
require their own specification and evaluation.
