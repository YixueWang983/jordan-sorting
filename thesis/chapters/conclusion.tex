\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:conclusion-summary}

This thesis turns the 1990 Simplified Jordan Sorting procedure into a shared
executable core with two concrete backends. Both maintain their own sorted
output and family state; neither receives the oracle's sorted answer. The
ordinary-list version makes representation costs visible, including a
quadratic insertion-copying lower bound. The real finger-tree version supplies
endpoint insertion, path-local split and header ownership transfer while
preserving existing pair identities and child groups. Failed updates terminate
the call and require the state to be discarded.

The theoretical and empirical contributions have separate support. The
source-linked argument reviews reversed-pointer representation, deletion
shortness, join ranks, mixed potentials and locality. The matched experiment
checks concrete implementations on qualified cases, with frozen batching,
recorded environment and independently reconstructed evidence. Neither the
proof receipt nor the timing curves substitute for the underlying argument.

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{sec:conclusion-questions}

\subsection{RQ1: Executable Reconstruction and Correctness}
\label{sec:conclusion-rq1}

Both backends execute the same Step~1/2/3 loop and independently recover sorted
output. The manual induction explains how neighbor selection, the two odd-stage
$z_1$ corrections, temporary transfer and adoption restore the output and exact
family grouping after each completed prefix. The physical argument relates
reversed ribs to a logical red--black tree and covers root/nonroot repairs,
metadata, endpoints and stable identities.

The two frozen input sets separately passed v2 qualification, which compares
independent synchronous checked states, adds a final fresh checked replay and
checks a fresh minimal execution. In formal measurement all 66,560 sample
calls and 1,520 warm-up calls returned correct outputs, with no failed cases.
These finite observations support the implementation; the general argument is
manual and source-specific, not a machine-checked proof or an external proof
certification. Valid-input sorting does not establish a recognition algorithm.

\subsection{RQ2: Observed Runtime Behavior}
\label{sec:conclusion-rq2}

Under the matched minimal-call scope, the tree is slower on all 140 common
inputs and all twelve targeted split inputs. The common-set median of
within-input tree/list ratios is 2.898, with range 1.114--3.535; the targeted
median is 3.180, with range 3.147--3.213. Size-stratum ratios generally narrow
at the larger common lengths, but no measured crossover or guaranteed future
crossover follows. Some timing cells have substantial spread and remain in
the results without exclusions.

The older rollback-enabled study compared Python sort and two different
pipeline scopes. Its numerical results remain historical, as summarized in
\cref{sec:results-historical}. They are not pooled with this experiment and
cannot estimate a causal benefit from removing rollback. The current study
answers the backend comparison directly but does not isolate each backend's
wrapping, allocation or checking costs.

\subsection{RQ3: Structure and Implementation Cost}
\label{sec:conclusion-rq3}

The source analysis supplies amortized constant endpoint insertion and
smaller-side logarithmic split using one potential across the mixed sequence.
A list-size potential then pays the split terms over the full sorting run.
Together with constant stage and ownership access, this gives linear total
source-level work under the stated unit-cost and expected/amortized dictionary
model. The ordinary-list backend retains its different copying and scan costs.

Measured wall times answer a different question. The common constructions
have no two-sided splits; the targeted group supplies that path at smaller
sides 3, 7 and 19, but still measures full sorting calls. Neither group identifies
an isolated split-cost function. The observed tree slowdown shows that the
reviewed operation bound does not ensure a practical advantage at these sizes
in this Python implementation. The historical counter correlations remain
exploratory associations, not a causal explanation for the current timings.

\section{Contributions and Remaining Work}
\label{sec:conclusion-contributions}
\label{sec:conclusion-future-work}

The resulting artifact combines an executable reconstruction, a real
heterogeneous backend, source-linked mathematical arguments, and a reproducible
comparison whose source, inputs, batch choices, failures and summaries are
inspectable. Separating certification, output recovery, physical storage and
untimed diagnostics makes both the algorithm and its evidence easier to audit.

Further work should broaden split shapes and lengths under predeclared rules,
check another recorded environment, and investigate constant overhead with a
separate profiling protocol. Such work should preserve the current negative
speed result rather than optimize until a preferred comparison appears.
Formalization or independent mathematical review could strengthen the manual
argument. Recognition, invalid-input behavior, error correction and polygon
clipping require separate specifications and experiments.

\section{Closing Perspective}
\label{sec:conclusion-closing}

The reconstruction now connects the incremental algorithm to the specialized
operations it needs, while exposing the limits of a concrete implementation.
The structural bound, finite correctness evidence and practical runtime are
three different conclusions. Keeping their assumptions and versions explicit
allows the thesis to report both the completed backend and its measured
slowdown without overstating either result.
