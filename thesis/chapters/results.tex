\section{Overall Comparison}
\label{sec:results-overall}
\label{sec:results-current}

The uninstrumented tree is faster on \UnifiedWins{} of the \UnifiedN{} measured
inputs; the ordinary list is faster on the remaining \UnifiedListWins{}.
There are no exact ties. Each input contributes one ratio $r_i$ of the tree's
median time to the list's median time, as defined in
\cref{sec:method-aggregation}. Only the second, complete attempt supplies these
results. The first attempt and the departure from the no-retry rule are
reported in \cref{sec:method-deviation}.

Giving every input equal weight, the median ratio is \UnifiedMedian{}, with
range \UnifiedMinimum{}--\UnifiedMaximum{}. This is a secondary description
of the five-source collection, whose composition is given in
\cref{tab:results-sources}; it is not an estimate of performance on an
application's input distribution. \Cref{fig:results-distribution} shows the
spread. A further descriptive count places \UnifiedNear{} inputs within
$0.98<r_i<1.02$. These inputs are approximately tied under this chosen band,
which was added after sampling. The band is not an equivalence test and does
not change the strict $r_i<1$ count above.

\section{Variation with Structure}
\label{sec:results-structure}

Structure is the clearest distinction within the common source.
\Cref{tab:results-nesting} compares its four predetermined categories.
Each category has the same representation across lengths, although the
strict-flat quota is smaller. The category medians decline from strict-flat
to heavy nesting. No strict-flat or low-nesting input favors the tree, whereas
tree-faster inputs occur in the medium and heavy categories. This is a
comparison of the selected constructions, not an experiment that varies
nesting depth while holding all other features fixed.

The heavy category contains the strongest tree advantages. Among its
\UnifiedHeavyN{} inputs, \UnifiedHeavyWins{} favor the tree. Every heavy input
has $S_i\leq1$, and none has a two-sided split; \UnifiedHeavyZero{} have no
split at all. Thus the advantage on these constructions does not require
frequent splitting. For the inputs without splits, it arises within the
remaining work of complete sorting. For inputs with one split, the total time
does not separate that split from the other operations. These measurements
cannot identify the contributions of insertion, navigation, ownership,
initialization or output recovery.

\section{Splitting and Complete-Sort Time}
\label{sec:results-targeted}
\label{sec:results-splits}

The targeted, balanced and skew inputs share the parameterized
\texttt{values\_for} construction. Within each measured $n$, their ratios
vary little across the selected $k$ values in
\cref{fig:results-splits}(a). At the larger lengths, however, only the skew
and balanced settings are available. Parameter, shape, size, parity and mirror
coverage are not balanced. These points do not identify a general effect of
$k$, and the repeated construction has no single comparable $k$.

The fraction $p_i=I_i/S_i$ also shows no clear monotonic relationship with the
ratio across this collection (\cref{fig:results-splits}(b)). An input with no
performed split has $p_i=\mathrm{NA}$, not zero. The figure therefore omits
\UnifiedNoSplit{} inputs from its horizontal axis while retaining them in
all main timing summaries. A fraction alone omits both the absolute number
and the sizes of the splits. Neither panel measures the cost of one split:
the timed call also includes construction, insertion, navigation, ownership
updates and output recovery.

\section{Results by Source}
\label{sec:results-sources}

All five sources completed qualification and formal sampling in the second
attempt. \Cref{tab:results-sources} reports their registration, deduplication
and completion counts alongside their timing summaries, as required by the
reporting protocol. Tree-faster inputs occur in common, geometric and seeded;
none occurs in targeted or structural. The inputs' generation rules and
coverage differ, so the source medians describe these collections rather
than ranking generators as populations.
