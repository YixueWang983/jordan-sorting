\chapter{Background}
\label{chap:background}

\section{Jordan Sequences and the Sorting Problem}
\label{sec:background-problem}

Let $C$ be a simple plane curve that crosses the $x$-axis wherever it meets
the axis. Listing the abscissas of the intersection points in the order in
which they occur along $C$ gives a sequence
\begin{equation}
  z_1,z_2,\ldots,z_n.
\end{equation}
Such a realizable sequence is called a \emph{Jordan sequence}. The Jordan
sorting problem is to recover the order of the same points along the
$x$-axis, that is, their increasing coordinate order, from their order along
the curve \parencite[p.~170]{hoffmann1986sorting}. The 1990 presentation uses
the term Jordan curve for both open and closed simple curves; the property
needed here is the absence of self-intersections
\parencite[pp.~85--86]{fung1990simplified}.

This formulation gives two distinct orders on one set of points:
\begin{description}
  \item[Curve order] is the input order $z_1,\ldots,z_n$ obtained by
    traversing $C$.
  \item[Axis order] is the output order obtained by moving from left to right
    along the $x$-axis.
\end{description}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=.92\textwidth]{jordan_sorting_problem.pdf}
  \caption[Jordan sorting from curve order to axis order.]{Illustration of the
  Jordan sorting problem. The simple curve encounters the five intersections
  in curve order $(p_1,p_5,p_2,p_4,p_3)$, whereas their axis order is
  $(p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4,p_5)$. Jordan sorting recovers the axis order from the
  curve order.}
  \label{fig:jordan-sorting-problem}
\end{figure}

The values are therefore not arbitrary keys presented in an arbitrary
permutation. They carry a geometric realizability constraint inherited from
the simplicity of $C$. The implementation studied in this thesis specializes
this model to distinct, mutually comparable coordinate values, as stated in
\cref{sec:implementation-input-model}.

The problem arose from geometric applications rather than from a desire to
replace general-purpose sorting. The 1986 paper relates it to ordering the
intersections of a simple polygon with a line and to ordering intersections
of two Jordan curves. The 1990 paper develops polygon clipping as its main
application and also notes the role of Jordan sorting in simple-polygon
triangulation
\parencites[pp.~170--171]{hoffmann1986sorting}[pp.~85--86]{fung1990simplified}.
In these settings, intersection
points can be obtained naturally in boundary or curve order, while the next
geometric stage requires their order along another line or boundary.

\section{The Structural Restriction Behind Jordan Sorting}
\label{sec:background-structure}

Assume, after reflection about the $x$-axis if necessary, that the first arc
lies above the axis. Consecutive intersections delimit alternating arcs above
and below it. The pairs
\begin{equation}
  \{z_1,z_2\},\{z_3,z_4\},\ldots
\end{equation}
belong to the upper side, while
\begin{equation}
  \{z_2,z_3\},\{z_4,z_5\},\ldots
\end{equation}
belong to the lower side. Each pair determines the open interval between its
two coordinates on the axis.

Two intervals from the same side cannot cross. If one endpoint of one such
pair lies strictly inside another pair's interval, then its other endpoint
must lie there as well; otherwise the corresponding arcs would intersect.
Consequently, same-side intervals are disjoint or nested. Replacing the
smaller endpoint by a left parenthesis and the larger by a right parenthesis
therefore produces one properly nested parenthesis system for each side
\parencite[pp.~172--173]{hoffmann1986sorting}.

The containment relation gives an ordered forest for each parity family. A
pair's parent is its smallest same-family enclosing pair, and siblings are
ordered by their left endpoints. Adding a dummy pair spanning
$(-\infty,+\infty)$ turns each forest into a rooted ordered tree. These are
the upper and lower family trees used by both historical algorithms
\parencites[p.~173]{hoffmann1986sorting}[p.~87]{fung1990simplified}. The full
notation and executable pair model used in this thesis are developed in
\cref{sec:algorithm-families}; here the important point is that simplicity
turns a geometric constraint into nested ordered structure.

This structure is what makes specialized algorithms possible for valid
inputs. While processing points in curve order, the algorithms maintain both
a sorted list of the processed coordinates and the two family trees.
Inserting the next pair into its family identifies a local position for the
new coordinate in the sorted list
\parencite[pp.~173--175]{hoffmann1986sorting}. The remaining difficulty is not
an unrestricted search through all processed values, but the efficient
maintenance and splitting of ordered family lists.

\section{The 1986 Linear-Time Algorithm}
\label{sec:background-1986}

\textcite{hoffmann1986sorting} gave an algorithm that both recognizes and
sorts Jordan sequences in $O(n)$ time. It processes the sequence from left to
right in curve order, maintaining a doubly linked sorted list and the upper
and lower family trees. When a new interval would violate the required
nesting, the recognition algorithm aborts; if all points are processed, the
maintained list is the sorted result \parencite[pp.~173--175]{hoffmann1986sorting}.

The central data-structural operation is splitting a family list around the
new interval. A circular doubly linked representation permits local insertion
but can require a scan proportional to the smaller side of a split. Across a
sequence of splits this yields an $O(n\log n)$ implementation, no better
asymptotically than general sorting \parencite[pp.~175--176]{hoffmann1986sorting}.
The paper obtains its linear bound by replacing those lists with circular
level-linked $(2,4)$ trees.

A level-linked $(2,4)$ tree stores list items at the leaves of a balanced
multiway search tree and connects nodes at equal height. These links support
finger searches whose cost depends on the shorter circular distance between
the starting item and the target. Together with tree splitting,
concatenation, and an amortized analysis, the representation supports the
family-list operations within $O(n)$ total time
\parencite[pp.~177--183]{hoffmann1986sorting}. Thus the 1986 result couples
two ideas:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item reduce Jordan recognition and sorting to incremental family-list
    manipulation; and
  \item realize that manipulation with a specialized level-linked data
    structure.
\end{enumerate}
The second component is essential to the published linear-time bound.

\section{The 1990 Simplification}
\label{sec:background-1990}

\textcite{fung1990simplified} retained the linear theoretical bound while
simplifying both the primary structure and the operations required of it. A
locality lemma shows that, after a pair has been added, the relevant children
of a family remain in one or two contiguous sibling lists. As a result, the
sorting algorithm needs only three abstract list operations:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item create a singleton list;
  \item insert an item at the front or back of a known list; and
  \item split a list at a specified item or key.
\end{enumerate}
In particular, the simplified formulation avoids the 1986 operation that
extracts a middle section and concatenates the two remaining ends
\parencite[pp.~86,88--90]{fung1990simplified}.

The algorithm again processes intersections incrementally. It maintains a
sorted list, upper and lower family trees, and sibling lists encoding the
local family order. Neighboring processed coordinates locate the relevant
same-family boundaries; insertion of the new pair and any necessary sibling
split then determine where the new point enters the sorted list. The exact
Step~1/2/3 reconstruction used in this project, including the symmetric
orientation and endpoint clarifications, is the subject of
\cref{chap:algorithm} and is not repeated here.

For the theoretical implementation, the 1990 paper proposes heterogeneous
finger trees. It states constant amortized time for singleton-list creation
and boundary insertion, and a logarithmic-in-the-smaller-side amortized bound
for splitting. A potential argument charges the split cost across the
operation sequence, giving $O(m)$ total time for $m$ list operations and
hence an $O(n)$ Jordan-sorting bound
\parencite[pp.~90--91]{fung1990simplified}. This is a theorem about the
algorithm together with that efficient list representation, not about every
concrete implementation of the three-operation interface.

The 1990 paper also states that the sorting method extends to recognition and
to the error-correcting Jordan-sorting task used in the polygon-triangulation
algorithm of \textcite{tarjan1988triangulating}, and that neither extension
changes its $O(n)$ bound \parencite[p.~91]{fung1990simplified}. The
error-correction task belongs to that separate triangulation work; neither it
nor recognition is evaluated in this thesis.

\section{Related Work and Positioning}
\label{sec:background-related-work}

\subsection{Ordered Lists and Finger Search}

The specialized list backends used for Jordan sorting belong to a broader
line of work on ordered-list representations with fingers and
locality-sensitive access. \Textcite{brown1978movable} study linear lists with
movable fingers, and \textcite{brown1980sorted} analyze a 2--3-tree
representation for sorted lists whose costs reflect locality of reference.
\Textcite{huddleston1982sorted} develop level-linked weak B-trees for arbitrary
sequences of sorted-list updates. The level-linked $(2,4)$ trees used by the
1986 Jordan-sorting algorithm are part of this historical data-structure
lineage, rather than a property of Jordan sequences alone.

These works provide context for the historical linear-time theorem, but they
do not describe the backend implemented in this thesis. The reconstruction
uses ordinary Python lists and therefore retains explicit scan, copy, slice,
and rebinding costs.

\subsection{Jordan Sorting and Geometric Applications}

The first symposium publication of the linear-time Jordan-sorting result
appeared in 1985 \parencite{hoffmann1985sorting}; the 1986 journal article used
throughout this thesis gives the expanded algorithm and data-structure account.
The later simplification connected Jordan sorting to polygon clipping
\parencite{fung1990simplified}, while the error-correcting task appears in the
polygon-triangulation algorithm of \textcite{tarjan1988triangulating}.
Subsequent work by \textcite{baryehuda1994triangulating} used a generalized
form of Jordan sorting together with point location and interval trees to
triangulate collections of disjoint Jordan polygonal chains.

This literature shows that Jordan sorting participates in a wider
computational-geometry research line, rather than being only an isolated
sorting problem. The present thesis nevertheless has a narrower target: it
reconstructs and evaluates valid-input sorting. It does not implement a new
theoretical list structure, polygon clipping, triangulation, error correction,
or recognition.

\section{Position and Scope of This Thesis}
\label{sec:background-thesis-scope}

This project separates three layers that are easy to conflate:
\begin{description}
  \item[Geometric validity.] A candidate sequence is checked against the
    project's Jordan-validity model before the paper core is timed.
  \item[Valid-input sorting.] The reconstructed paper control flow maintains
    its own partial order and recovers the final output from that state.
  \item[List representation.] Sibling lists are implemented with ordinary
    Python lists rather than either historical theoretical backend.
\end{description}

Accordingly, the empirical study concerns sorting of oracle-certified valid
inputs. Recognition remains separate, as specified in
\cref{sec:method-scope}. The paper core does not consume oracle-sorted output,
whereas the simplified reference pipeline deliberately remains oracle-backed;
the implementation boundary is detailed in
\cref{sec:implementation-certification}.

The ordinary-list representation supports an executable and auditable
reconstruction, but it may scan, slice, copy, and rebind list contents. This
thesis therefore does not claim to implement the level-linked trees of 1986
or the heterogeneous finger trees of 1990, and it does not transfer the
papers' linear-time theorem to the concrete Python backend. The consequences
for empirical interpretation are stated in
\cref{sec:limitations-backend,sec:limitations-timing-scopes}.
