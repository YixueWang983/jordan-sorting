\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion-summary}

This thesis reconstructs Simplified Jordan Sorting as one executable core with
ordinary-list and heterogeneous finger-tree backends. Both recover the sorted
output from their maintained state. The implementation connects the paper's
operations to a source-level cost argument and a reproducible runtime comparison.

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{sec:conclusion-questions}
\label{sec:conclusion-rq1}

\textbf{RQ1: reconstruction and correctness.}
The shared Step~1/2/3 loop maintains geometric ownership, exact sibling groups
and sorted prefixes. The argument in \cref{app:backend-argument} explains the
two odd-stage $z_1$ corrections, temporary transfer, reversed ribs and recursive
repair transients. Stable pair identities and child groups survive splitting.
All \UnifiedN{} inputs passed the experiment's equivalence and qualification
checks, and every saved output in the \UnifiedBatches{} formal sample batches
of the complete attempt agrees with its expected sorted order. These checks
support the reconstruction on the tested inputs without replacing its argument.

\label{sec:conclusion-rq2}
\textbf{RQ2: observed runtime.}
The uninstrumented tree is faster on \UnifiedWins{} of \UnifiedN{} inputs;
the ordinary list is faster on the remainder. The input-level tree/list ratio
has median \UnifiedMedian{} and range \UnifiedMinimum{}--\UnifiedMaximum{}.
The pooled median decreases across the measured size bands, and the common
source's more heavily nested categories have lower ratios. These observations
apply to the selected families and lengths up to \UnifiedMaxN{} on one host.
The main statistics use only the complete second attempt; the first power
failure and the departure from the no-retry rule remain disclosed.

\label{sec:conclusion-rq3}
\textbf{RQ3: structure and cost.}
One potential pays propagation across mixed updates, giving amortized constant
endpoint insertion and smaller-side logarithmic split. A list-size potential
then pays the split terms over the full run, including bounded ownership and
shared-core work. The linear total uses unit-cost comparisons and machine-word
operations, expected dictionary lookup and amortized growth. Ordinary-list
insertion copying alone has a quadratic lower bound.

The five sources cover different splitting histories, including two-sided
splits in targeted, structural, geometric and seeded inputs. Yet the strongest
tree advantages occur among heavily nested common inputs with at most one
split and no two-sided split. Within the shared targeted/balanced/skew
construction, ratios vary little with the selected smaller-side parameter at
a fixed length. Complete-sort time does not isolate a split's cost or explain
which remaining operations account for the tree's advantages.

\section{Contributions and Further Work}
\label{sec:conclusion-contributions}
\label{sec:conclusion-future-work}
\label{sec:conclusion-closing}

The thesis provides an implementation whose operations can be traced to the
reconstruction and cost argument, together with reproducible measurements.
Several extensions would test how far the observed trends carry. Generators
that combine depth with long sibling lists, and application-derived inputs
with large interior splits, would address structural gaps in the current
families. Open polygonal chains would add odd lengths, while multiple starts
and directions per polygon would test dependence on traversal choice.

Larger inputs, including the excluded length-1500 cases, would test whether
the narrowing of the tree/list ratio continues beyond $n=\UnifiedMaxN{}$.
Intersection sequences from real polygon data would connect the measurements
to the clipping application of Fung et al.\ Operation counts from the
instrumented tree could describe differences in work mix and guide targeted
measurements of candidate explanations; they cannot alone assign runtime
contributions. Independent review of the finite-parent locality extension and
the mixed-operation potential argument would strengthen confidence in the
cost analysis. Recognition, invalid-input behavior and error correction each
require their own specification and evaluation.
