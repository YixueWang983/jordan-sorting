\chapter{Limitations}
\label{chap:limitations}

\section{Scope and Configuration}
\label{sec:limitations-scope}
\label{sec:limitations-version}

The results compare two concrete Python implementations: ordinary lists and
the uninstrumented finger tree, both using the minimal sorting core. Source
code, inputs and batch sizes stayed unchanged between preparation and the
complete formal session. Earlier instrumented and rollback-enabled studies
have different timing scopes and environments and are not pooled with these
results. In the current implementation, an update failure may leave a partial
state that must be discarded; invariants apply to completed updates.

\section{Mathematical Model and Backend Fidelity}
\label{sec:limitations-backend}

The physical and locality arguments have passed this project's source-level
manual review. They have not been machine formalized or independently certified
by a separate external reviewer. The endpoint and first-point rules are
executable interpretations of details left implicit in the paper. The
finite-parent locality case is an argument developed for this implementation;
the published lemma covers the latest-child case. The mixed-operation
potential argument also remains open to independent review.

The tree's linear total bound uses unit-cost comparisons and machine-word
operations with expected dictionary lookup and amortized growth. Arbitrary
Python comparison methods, large-number bit costs, interpreter scheduling and
wall-clock behavior are outside it. Complete state audits and replay are also
outside the minimal complexity claim. Changes to the implementation require
reassessing the source-level argument as well as rerunning affected tests.

Ordinary lists do not inherit that bound: insertion copying alone is quadratic
on increasing inputs, and their membership, ancestry and ownership work differs.
The runtime comparison includes these differences in implementation. Timing
curves alone establish neither complexity statement. The experiment's
instrumented/uninstrumented equivalence check covers the \UnifiedN{} registered
inputs; it is finite evidence, not a general proof about the source transformation.

\section{Input Coverage and Scale}
\label{sec:limitations-sizes}
\label{sec:limitations-generality}

The \UnifiedN{} inputs come from five families chosen to vary nesting,
sibling-list structure and interior splitting. They are controlled
constructions, not a uniform sample from the space of valid Jordan sequences.
Common uses fixed category quotas; targeted and part of structural share one
parameterized construction. Geometric uses one grid-tree polygon generator,
and seeded uses gap-selection heuristics. Mirrors, shared random streams and
related parameter settings are dependent. The mixed median and faster-input
count depend on this collection's composition.

The families leave structural gaps. Nesting depth and sibling-list length
are not varied independently in a systematic design: a heavily nested suffix
and an increasing flat construction test different operation histories.
Balanced constructions deliberately include larger interior splits, while the
geometric family's observed smaller side never exceeds
\UnifiedGeometricSmallMax{} pair entries. The targeted and structural
constructions provide only selected sizes and update histories, with parity
and direction coupled in the structural source. They do not cover arbitrary
combinations of deep nesting, long sibling lists and large repeated splits.

The geometric source contains only even lengths. A closed simple polygon in
general position crosses a line an even number of times, and this generator
uses only one sampled start and direction per polygon. Its fixed grid height
also changes the aspect ratio as length grows. None of the inputs comes from
an application dataset of real polygons undergoing clipping.

The largest measured length is \UnifiedMaxN{}; the \UnifiedExcluded{} seeded
inputs of length 1500 were excluded before sampling. Their earlier correctness
records do not provide runtime evidence for this experiment. The lower ratios
on larger and more heavily nested constructions describe these families over
the measured range. They do not establish a crossover or continued improvement
above that range, or the same behavior for other generators.

\section{Timing Scopes and Environment}
\label{sec:limitations-timing-scopes}
\label{sec:limitations-execution}

The timed call includes construction, the shared algorithm, output recovery
and batching overhead. The backends retain different local checks,
allocations and ownership work. The uninstrumented tree also binds methods
when each instance is created. Complete-sort measurements do not isolate
individual operation costs, including the cost of a split and the operations
behind the tree's advantage on deeply nested inputs. Split fractions and
sizes omit other parts of the work history, and operation counts describe the
work mix without assigning runtime shares. The earlier instrumentation
diagnostic likewise covers its selected inputs without assigning an additive
share of total algorithm cost. The constructor microbenchmark describes its
own fixed batch design, including automatic GC and object retention; its
comparison with the shortest sorting median provides a scale reference,
not an isolated binding cost or a correction to sorting time.

The complete formal session uses one Apple M4 machine, macOS 27.2 and
CPython 3.12.14 with AC power and low-power mode disabled. CPU affinity was
not fixed, and actual core scheduling was not recorded. Warm-ups and
alternating order do not establish stable frequency, temperature or absence
of unrelated system activity. The restart is documented in
\cref{sec:method-deviation}.

Sources ran in a fixed sequence. Session drift can therefore be confounded
with source, while alternation balances only the backend order within an
input. The prior cross-host diagnostic informed host selection; it does not
make this single-host experiment a cross-platform performance study.

\section{Statistical and Exploratory Reach}
\label{sec:limitations-statistics}
\label{sec:limitations-exploratory}

\UnifiedRounds{} batches describe repeat variability for each fixed
input/backend. Dividing duration by B does not yield B independent samples.
Across inputs, median relative IQR is \UnifiedListIQRMedian{}\% for the list
and \UnifiedTreeIQRMedian{}\% for the tree; the maxima are
\UnifiedListIQRMax{}\% and \UnifiedTreeIQRMax{}\%, respectively. All batches
remain included. The causes of the larger spreads were not measured, and
variation cannot be assigned to CPU scheduling from these records.

Cross-input ranges and quartiles describe a collection of input summaries,
not uncertainty intervals for a universal effect. The histogram and the
2\% near-equality band are post hoc descriptive additions. The band is a
descriptive threshold, not an equivalence test. The comparison of
\UnifiedStabilityN{} overlapping inputs from the two attempts is also post hoc,
outside the protocol and outside the main statistics; its observed changes do
not establish equivalence. No significance tests, independent-sample confidence
intervals or causal conclusions are drawn from these dependent constructions.

\section{Output Provenance and Recognition}
\label{sec:limitations-provenance}

External certification and expected sorting support qualification and output
checking for the paper cores. The reference pipeline instead returns
oracle-derived output, as described in \cref{sec:implementation-scope}.

All formal inputs are pre-certified valid. The evidence does not establish
recognition accuracy, invalid-input failure reasons or recognition runtime.
The sorting proof likewise starts from its validity precondition. Error
correction and geometric applications remain outside this evaluation.
